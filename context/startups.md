# Philosophy on Startups

## Foundational Beliefs

### Startups as Experiments
A startup is a hypothesis about value creation. The goal is to test that hypothesis as quickly and cheaply as possible. Most hypotheses are wrong - the skill is in learning fast and adapting.

### Problem-First Thinking
Great startups start with problems, not solutions. Fall in love with the problem, not your solution. The best founders can articulate the problem more clearly than anyone else.

### Speed is a Feature
In startups, speed is not just about efficiency - it's a competitive advantage. The ability to learn and adapt faster than competitors often matters more than being right initially.

## On Building Products

### Talk to Users
There is no substitute for direct user contact. Data and analytics are useful, but they can't replace understanding the human context of how people actually use your product.

### Minimum Viable Products
MVP doesn't mean "crappy version" - it means the smallest thing that tests your core hypothesis. Often that's smaller than you think. Sometimes it's a landing page. Sometimes it's a manual process.

### Features vs. Value
Shipping features is easy. Creating value is hard. Always ask: does this actually solve a problem users have? Will they pay for it (with money or attention)?

### Design for the Core Use Case
Build for your best users, not your edge cases. A product that's amazing for one type of user beats a product that's mediocre for everyone.

## On Scaling

### Do Things That Don't Scale
Early on, manual and unscalable approaches teach you what to build. Premature optimization is the enemy of learning.

### Know When to Scale
There's a phase transition when you need to shift from doing things manually to building systems. Recognizing this inflection point is crucial. Too early and you waste resources; too late and you break under load.

### Culture Scales Before Process
In early stages, culture - shared values and decision-making frameworks - scales better than process. Hire people who can make good decisions without explicit rules.

### Technical Debt is Real Debt
Technical debt has interest payments. Sometimes taking on debt is the right call, but be honest about the costs. Don't pretend shortcuts are free.

## On Fundraising

### Raise When You Don't Need To
The best time to raise money is when you don't desperately need it. Desperation kills negotiating leverage and leads to bad deals.

### Money is Not Success
Raising money is not a milestone - it's taking on an obligation. Every dollar raised is a dollar you need to turn into more value. Celebrate customers, not checks.

### Right Investors Matter
The right investor is a partner, not just a check. They bring networks, expertise, and support during hard times. The wrong investor can be worse than no investor.

## On Team

### Hire Slow, Fire Fast
This is cliché because it's true. Bad hires are incredibly expensive - not just in salary but in opportunity cost, cultural damage, and management overhead.

### Diversity of Thought
Homogeneous teams have blind spots. You want people who see problems differently, not just people who look different but think the same.

### Ownership Over Tasks
Give people ownership of outcomes, not lists of tasks. The best work comes from people who feel genuine responsibility for results.

### Transparency Builds Trust
Default to sharing information. Secrets and information hoarding create political environments. Trust your team with context.

## On Failure

### Failure is Data
Failed experiments provide information. The goal is to fail fast and learn. Punishing failure creates cultures that avoid risk and stop learning.

### Most Startups Fail
This is just reality. The skill is in maximizing learning per dollar spent and maintaining enough runway to find product-market fit.

### Know When to Quit
Persistence is valuable, but so is recognizing when the hypothesis is disproven. Pivot or shut down - don't zombie along.
